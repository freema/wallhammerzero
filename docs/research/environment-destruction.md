# Destroy parts, regenerate rubble, discard debris

Wallhammer Zero should support destruction as a **change of state on generated parts, not as stored geometry**. A destroyed wall becomes "part P of building B is in damage state k", a few bytes in the D40/D79 edit layer. The generator turns that state back into geometry, fracture pieces and a settled rubble pile, so none of that geometry is ever saved, and physics debris is a short-lived cosmetic overlay that nothing else reads. Every shipped system surveyed makes the same split. Battlefield, Red Faction, The Finals, Teardown, Unreal Chaos and NVIDIA Blast all keep a small, discrete, authoritative structural state (part health, broken bonds, removed voxels) apart from large, disposable debris. Teardown, the closest precedent, replicates destruction as integer commands and saves full 80 MB world snapshots. Wallhammer can keep Teardown's command model and drop the snapshots, because its world is regenerated anyway. The procedural angle adds one real advantage. A split grammar already *knows* the chunks, the contact faces between them, the load-bearing roles and the ground anchors that Blast and Chaos have to reconstruct from meshes. No published work was found that exploits this, so it is original design rather than established practice. The cost of getting it wrong is front-loaded. If the M3b building generator does not emit stable per-part IDs, a part graph and a per-part state input, destruction later means rewriting the generator, the save format and the cache keys. Those three things are cheap now. Everything else (a tier A structural solver, seeded fracture, debris, nav rebuild, rendering masks, audio) belongs in a later destruction milestone. The evidence has holes. No studio has published frame budgets, debris caps, navmesh handling or a save format for destruction in a regenerated open world, and several commonly repeated facts rest on press paraphrase or forums. Those are flagged below.

## Every shipped system splits a small authoritative state from disposable debris

The common pattern is easier to see across all titles at once than one by one.

| System | Geometry model | Authoritative state | Debris | Persistence | Multiplayer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Red Faction: Guerrilla (2009) | pre-split hidden "shards" per material, buildings only, no terrain | stress check on supports | rigid bodies | "world anchoring" decides what stays; unknown save format | 16 players |
| Battlefield 6 / Frostbite (2025) | authored parts with health and "health transitions" | part health | prioritized and culled on the network | ruins and debris piles stay for the match | server, 64 players |
| The Finals (UE5, 2023–2026) | structural "Strain" layer plus physics, plus an elastic layer since Oct 2026 | server-side | server-side | per match | server-authoritative |
| Teardown | voxel volumes, no triangles | voxel removal in fixed-point integers | state-synced rigid bodies | full RLE snapshot, ~80 MB | deterministic commands plus state sync |
| Unreal Chaos | pre-fractured geometry collections with cluster levels | strain thresholds per level on a connection graph | removed on sleep | not covered | corrections stop below an "abandon" cluster level |
| NVIDIA Blast | hierarchical chunks plus a support graph of bonds | bond health, iterative stress solver | none (physics-agnostic) | POD memory, direct binary serialization | not covered |

Frostbite is the most explicit about the split. Since Bad Company, destroying part of a house removed a piece of geometry, added detail meshes around the hole, applied a destruction mask and spawned particles and mesh debris ([Kihl, SIGGRAPH 2010](https://www.advances.realtimerendering.com/s2010/Kihl-Destruction%20in%20Frostbite(SIGGRAPH%202010%20Advanced%20RealTime%20Rendering%20Course).pdf)). Battlefield 6 kept that part-based model. It reworked the health transition system so game teams can hook into state changes, overhauled "how destruction events are networked" with "prioritization and culling of part destruction and debris spawning" for 64 players, and made ruins persist: "We wanted the result of destruction to stay, creating interesting ruins and level-designed debris piles" ([EA](https://www.ea.com/news/how-battlefield-6-redefined-destruction)). This is a general-purpose fracture solver only in the marketing. Under the hood it is discrete part states, which are cheap to network and persist. The EA article is official but reads as marketing and gives no numbers.

Red Faction: Guerrilla shows both the appeal and the price of pre-fractured content. Volition stored "invisible shards that are in the buildings that don't get activated until destruction has happened", sized per material ("concrete will have a different size from fiberglass"). Once the shard density was so high "you could write your name into the wall", which produced hundreds of thousands of polygons ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-destructible-world-building-i-red-faction-armageddon-i-)). Memory was the binding constraint: "We were literally stealing memory from other systems just to do basic functionality", and textures were downgraded to pay for it ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2024/02/02/video-games/destruction-video-games-battlefield-bad-company-red-faction-battlebit-teardown-the-finals)). A generator that produces fracture pieces from `(part uid, seed)` on demand removes exactly that storage cost. Guerrilla also dropped terrain destruction, which the 2001 Geo-Mod had done by CSG-subtracting a low-poly explosion shape. That earlier approach produced "weird little tiny triangles that would go all the way through the world" and was limited by rationing rockets ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2024/02/02/video-games/destruction-video-games-battlefield-bad-company-red-faction-battlebit-teardown-the-finals)).

Teardown is the precedent that matches Wallhammer's rules most closely. Everything is voxels on a regular grid, so destruction is discrete. Dennis Gustafsson rewrote destruction in **fixed-point integer math** so it could be replicated as commands such as "cut hole in this shape at voxel x,y,z", while transforms, velocities and players use unreliable state sync within "around one Mbit per client". An earlier attempt that sent changed voxel data choked the connection on large objects. Join-in-progress replays the command stream, the buffer for it is capped, and join is disabled when it fills ([Gustafsson, Mar 2026](https://blog.voxagon.se/2026/03/13/teardown-multiplayer.html); corroborated by [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/teardown-developer-breaks-down-multiplayer-and-voxel-destruction-tech)). One of the three research passes got a 404 on that blog URL, so its details rest on one fetch plus the interview. Saves go the other way: Gustafsson stores the whole world "for robustness", run-length encoded to 15–20 % of the raw size, which gives **~80 MB quicksaves** on large levels of about half a billion voxels. Derived data (physics voxels, culling and lighting acceleration structures) is "100% reproducible from the main voxel data" and rebuilt on load ([Voxagon, 2020](https://blog.voxagon.se/2020/11/18/teardown-quicksave.html)). In Wallhammer the main data itself is reproducible, so the save shrinks to the command log or its compacted form.

The Finals proves that a server can own full building collapse for every player: "All of the destruction is taking place on servers Embark Studios controls… it will look the same for all players in a match" ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/i-almost-dont-believe-the-destructibility-in-this-fps-is-real)). Its limits are design limits: "there is a ground level that you can't destroy", and "if it breaks in one area, it needs to break in another area as well" ([MP1st](https://mp1st.com/news/the-finals-interview-embark-talks-balancing-destruction-and-more)). The October 2026 update added a third, elastic layer on top of structural "Strain" and debris physics, so floors sag instead of dropping whole ([KitGuru](https://www.kitguru.net/gaming/joao-silva/embark-studios-reveals-major-destruction-system-update-for-the-finals/)). **Every mechanism detail for The Finals is second-hand.** Embark has published no engineering talk, and the claim that it uses "off-the-shelf" UE5 destruction is a journalist's wording. Crackdown 3 is the cautionary tale of the server route. It ran "Havok in Azure" on the equivalent of "12 Xbox Ones" for "lockstep full destruction", and only in one multiplayer mode, while the campaign kept a fixed world ([Windows Central](https://windowscentral.com/inside-crackdown-3s-azure-cloud-powered-destruction)).

The middleware converges on one data model. Chaos builds geometry collections with cluster levels and a damage threshold per level, warns "you might not want thousands of pieces on the initial break", anchors pieces with fields that must be applied before simulation because "the connection graph must also be established prior to simulation", and removes debris after a sleep threshold ([Epic, UE 4.27](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/chaos-destruction-overview?application_version=4.27); [Fields guide](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/chaos-fields-user-guide-in-unreal-engine)). Blast's low-level layer is a "C-style API consisting of stateless functions" that do not "spawn tasks, allocate, or deallocate memory", with a "portable memory layout" for memcpy cloning and binary serialization. It has no physics or graphics representation of its own ([Blast docs](https://github.com/NVIDIAGameWorks/Blast/blob/master/docs/index.rst)). That is the closest existing design to Wallhammer's C17, arena and handle rules. **Jolt has no fracture module**, and its author says a fracture demo "is not going to happen anytime soon" ([Jolt #1722](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics/discussions/1722)). It does supply the primitives: a `MutableCompoundShape` that "can be constructed/changed at runtime", a breakable-constraint pattern, and cross-platform determinism at about 8 % cost ([Jolt docs](https://jrouwe.github.io/JoltPhysics/index.html)).

The scope limits are as consistent as the architecture. The Finals and Teardown keep the ground or bedrock indestructible, Guerrilla left terrain alone, and Bad Company 2 kept girders and reinforced concrete standing ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2024/02/02/video-games/destruction-video-games-battlefield-bad-company-red-faction-battlebit-teardown-the-finals)). No shipped game in the notes destroys buildings across a large regenerated open world and saves the result. That combination is new territory for Wallhammer.

## A split grammar already knows what Blast reconstructs from meshes

Shipped games almost all **pre-fracture** offline and decide only *which* bonds break at runtime. Real runtime geometric fracture exists in research and costs milliseconds per event. Müller and colleagues' 2001 quasi-static method needed 10–80 ms of static analysis for 1,000–4,000 tetrahedra on a 400 MHz workstation ([Müller et al. 2001](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/fracture.pdf)). The 2013 VACD method aligns artist-made fracture templates with the impact point and intersects them with a convex decomposition ([SIGGRAPH archive](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=108389)); its paper could not be retrieved, so its timings are unverified. "Breaking Good" (2022) precomputes fracture modes and projects each impact onto them, giving impact-dependent patterns "without online crack propagation simulation" ([arXiv 2111.05249](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.05249)). The production middleware sits on a fragile base. Blast's fracture tool needs closed meshes "without self-intersections or T-junctions", otherwise results are "unpredictable", and it ships a mesh cleaner and an approximate "Average" bond mode to cope with real assets ([Blast authoring](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/blast/docs/api/extensions/ext_authoring.html)).

For a code-generated world the trade-off changes shape. "Pre-fracture" can run inside the generator, so the real choice is between fracturing at generation time (tier A, cached) and fracturing at impact time. The grammar also removes the hardest step, rebuilding structure from triangles. Blast has a dedicated `buildDescFromInternalFracture` path precisely because a tool that made the cuts already knows the interfaces ([Blast authoring](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/blast/docs/api/extensions/ext_authoring.html)). A CGA split knows them too: every split along an axis creates a shared rectangle between two scopes, and every terminal knows whether it is a column, a slab, an infill wall or an ornament.

The recommendation is that `eng_gen_arch` emits a **part graph (`destruct_desc`) next to the render mesh**, as a tier A output that is hashed and part of the Merkle key. Nodes are grammar terminals or kit parts. Each carries a stable `uid64` derived from the building's anchor and the grammar path (an input-space anchor under D79, never output order), a material id, fixed-point mass and centroid, the convex pieces already known from the scope boxes, a role, an anchor flag for parts touching the foundation or terrain, and the number of damage states it supports. Bonds are the shared faces between adjacent scopes, with contact area and axis, the inputs a stress solver needs for separate compression, tension and shear limits. Roles set default bond strengths and mark infill and ornament as non-structural, so removing them never triggers collapse. Blast's own model needs exactly these fields: per-node mass, volume and position, plus static nodes for the world ([Blast stress solver](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/blast/docs/api/extensions/ext_stress.html)). Bonds to non-destructible geometry can be marked permanent, as Omniverse Blast allows ([Omniverse Blast](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/app_code/prod_extensions/ext_blast.html)).

Damage states per part replace free-form fracture with a ladder the generator understands:

| State | Meaning | How it is produced | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 intact | normal part | normal grammar | none |
| 1 cracked | visual damage only | shader mask and vertex damage channel | one small buffer update |
| 2 breached | hole at the impact cell | re-evaluate the part with the grammar's opening rule at the impact cell | one part regeneration, no booleans |
| 3 fractured | part split into chunks | seeded convex clipping of the part's pieces | lazy generation, cached |
| 4 gone | part removed, rubble assigned | part dropped from the mesh, rubble archetype placed | one part regeneration |

State 2 is the cheap trick specific to procedural content. The rule that cuts a window into a wall can cut a breach into it, so a hole needs no boolean on an arbitrary mesh. State 3 stays cheap because kit parts are mostly boxes and extrusions. Clipping a convex polyhedron by the bisector planes of a Voronoi cell, cell by cell as Voro++ does ([voro++](https://github.com/chr1shr/voro)), needs only add, subtract, multiply and divide. It can be written under the tier A rules in fixed-point, or in IEEE floats with `-ffp-contract=off` and a fixed operation order. Non-convex parts such as an arched wall are already unions of convex sub-scopes in the grammar, which is a free convex decomposition in the VACD sense. The fracture seed is `hash(generator_version, part_uid64, damage_state, impact_cell)`, with sites drawn from a named `"fracture"` RNG stream and biased towards the impact. Material picks the pattern: radial and concentric for glass, clustered Voronoi for concrete, slices along the grain for wood, and cells snapped to the facade generator's brick grid for brick. Siege and Control, the two shipped examples of procedural fracture on authored surfaces, are both material-driven ([Ubisoft interview](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/4GHX2yepSaKkflLjLAlpwO/the-art-of-destruction-in-rainbow-six-siege-an-interview-with-julien-lheureux); [GDC Vault, Control](https://gdcvault.com/play/1030643/Destructible-Environments-in-Control-Lessons)). Cut faces come out of the clipper as cap polygons, tagged with an interior material from the recipe (brick core, concrete with rebar) and given triplanar or box-projected UVs, so nothing needs unwrapping at runtime.

Manifold, already planned in `eng_gen_mesh`, is the tool for genuinely non-convex cuts and for rocks and terrain modelled as SDFs. It requires manifold input and claims to be the first robust mesh boolean ([Manifold README](https://github.com/elalish/manifold)), but its cross-platform bit identity is unverified, and its speed claims are self-reported. Its use in destruction should wait for the existing ROADMAP check "bit identity of Manifold". Blast itself is BSD-3 licensed but C++, ships build scripts only for Windows and Linux, and says nothing about determinism ([NVIDIA-Omniverse/PhysX](https://github.com/NVIDIA-Omniverse/PhysX)). It is a reference design, not a dependency. Teardown-style voxels for everything would mean a different renderer and conflict with the triangle pipeline and D5. Voxels belong to the existing "Voxel volumes" item for digging and caves, not to building destruction.

## Tier A decides what breaks, Jolt only moves the pieces

Structural integrity comes in three strengths: connectivity only (an island no longer connected to an anchor falls), heuristic load paths, and iterative stress solvers. Blast's solver works "directly with the bond graph", spreading forces iteratively with separate tension, compression and shear limits, and its cost is "linearly proportional to `maxSolverIterationsPerFrame`". Gravity needs at least one fixed node, otherwise the structure behaves as if in free fall ([Blast stress solver](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/blast/docs/api/extensions/ext_stress.html)). Omniverse adds an elastic limit, where damage starts, and a fatal limit, where the bond breaks at once ([Omniverse Blast](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/app_code/prod_extensions/ext_blast.html)). Teardown launched with no structural check at all ("entire buildings will often be held up by one voxel") and only showed a structural-integrity prototype in September 2026 ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/this-real-time-voxel-building-demolition-is-so-satisfying/)).

All of this is graph work on small graphs, hundreds to a few thousand nodes per building, so it fits tier A. The recommended solver lives in the tier A logic of `eng_sim`. On every broken bond it runs a breadth-first search from anchor nodes over unbroken bonds, visiting nodes in ascending `uid64`, at O(nodes + bonds). It then runs a Jacobi-style load propagation in Q16.16 or integer milli-newtons with a **fixed iteration count rather than "until converged"**, so the result is identical on every machine. Elastic limits advance a part's damage state, and fatal limits break the bond. The solver runs only on the dirty subgraph of buildings touched this tick, in building-id order, at the fixed step. Its output (bonds to break, islands to detach, parts that change state) is the whole gameplay-relevant result. No published benchmark of Blast's solver exists, so the iteration count and the per-building part cap have to come from measurement 31.

Jolt stays out of that loop for documented reasons. Its broadphase queries are not deterministic, narrowphase results arrive in varying order, contact listeners are called "from multiple threads in nondeterministic order", and cross-platform identity holds only if API calls happen in exactly the same order ([Jolt Architecture.md](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics/blob/master/Docs/Architecture.md)). `RestoreState` breaks if bodies were added or removed in the rolled-back frames ([Jolt docs](https://jrouwe.github.io/JoltPhysics/index.html)), and destruction adds and removes bodies constantly. Jolt's author also advises that in a partially synced scene, unsynced destructibles must be kinematic towards synced bodies, "or else they will affect the simulation and break determinism" ([Jolt #1254](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics/discussions/1254)). The inputs to the solver therefore come from gameplay events with tier A parameters (weapon, explosion radius and energy), not from Jolt contact impulses. Hits are resolved against the convex pieces in `destruct_desc` by a tier A ray and sphere test, not by a Jolt raycast. If a Jolt query is used, its result is sorted and quantized before it touches anything tier A.

The canonical record is a **destruction event**: `{tick, building_uid64, part_uid64, impact_cell, impact_dir, energy, damage_class}`. The impact cell is the part-local position quantized to about 1 cm in three int16s, and energy is fixed-point. It is the consequence of a `player_cmd` or of tier A simulation (an AI artillery strike), never of physics. Everything else is a pure function of the event and the generator: the new damage states, the fracture geometry, the bonds of the new chunks and the rubble. This is Teardown's command model applied to parts instead of voxels.

The awkward step is the transition from falling pieces to a resting pile. Jolt's final debris positions cannot be part of tier A, and even under `CROSS_PLATFORM_DETERMINISTIC` they depend on call order and on measurement 27. So the persistent result has to be decided **at the moment of the event, not when the debris settles**. When a part reaches state 4, tier A picks a rubble archetype and footprint from `(part uid, seed, fall direction)`. That footprint is what navigation, cover, lanes and saves see. Jolt debris then plays out as presentation and, once asleep or past a lifetime, is swapped for the regenerated rubble mesh, using the dissolve-on-removal that Chaos ships for the same purpose ([UE Python API](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/python-api/class/GeometryCollection?application_version=5.4)). The visual pile and the physical debris will not match exactly. Dust, a short fade and a debris lifetime cap hide that, and it is the price of keeping physics out of tier A. Large chunks that should block a door are the one grey zone. They can be tier A kinematic objects placed by the same rubble rule, or tier B1 bodies whose rest state the authority sends, as The Finals does. Which one depends on measurement 27. Debris bodies are created in sorted `uid64` order with engine-assigned IDs (`CreateBodyWithID`), on a separate object layer that gameplay never reads.

## Consequences run through every subsystem

### Persistence and streaming: destruction is one more edit layer

Shipped games fall into three persistence patterns. Teardown stores the full world. No Man's Sky reportedly caps terrain edits per save and lets the procedural base reassert itself when the cap overflows, which players experience as their bases being buried ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/275850/discussions/0/3826424998948955516)). **That source is weak**, player forums with no Hello Games statement. The third pattern resets on purpose. Bethesda's cells respawn after a timer unless flagged "Never Resets" ([UESP](https://cs.uesp.net/wiki/Cell_Reset), community wiki); Just Cause 3's bridges "respawn after you've blown them up" ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/wreaking-havoc-in-just-cause-3s-playground-of-explosions-do-not-publish/2)); Cities: Skylines turns collapsed buildings into rubble that services clear and the city rebuilds ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/natural-disasters-lets-you-destroy-your-cities-skylines-cities-but-also-save-them)).

Wallhammer should store destruction as a **state edit keyed by `(owner cell, part uid64)`**: damage state, impact cell and seed. The rubble, the fracture pieces and the derived collision are regenerated, as Teardown regenerates its acceleration structures. That makes the building generator's context struct take a per-part state vector as an input, exactly as D39 makes player roads and zones generator inputs. A damaged building is then just another cache key in the Merkle cache, and streaming a destroyed block out and back in is an ordinary regeneration. The D79 rules apply unchanged. A state edit whose part vanished after a grammar change goes to the orphan queue. Unlike a moved lamp, a destroyed wall should have a coarsening fallback (a "building ruined fraction" or a ruin archetype for the whole building) so that a domain bump never silently rebuilds a ruin. That is the No Man's Sky failure in another form.

The event log needs compaction. Teardown's join buffer fills and disables join-in-progress, and an unbounded per-cell log would do the same to load times. Events fold into per-part states once applied, and only the folded state is saved. Measurement 17 (replay time of a heavily edited cell) already exists; destruction gives it a worst case. The cell also needs a policy flag (`persist`, `reset_after(t)`, `rebuild_by_sim`) so that the city mode can heal, the strategy mode can clear and rebuild through its simulation, and a planet can keep everything. Any per-cell or per-save edit budget needs a visible overflow policy: merge into a coarser ruin state, convert to an authored ruin archetype, or refuse the edit.

One timing detail matters for D46. In the strategy mode the global simulation ticks everywhere, and an off-screen artillery strike can hit a building whose cell is not loaded. If the part graph exists only in the MESHED cell state, that strike must wait for streaming. The graph should therefore be produced at the LAYOUT state, which already feeds coarse collision in M7, so tier A destruction can run without meshes.

### Simulation and navigation: rubble edits the graph, debris never does

Navigation is where the evidence is thinnest. No shipped title in the notes documents how its navmesh or AI reacts to collapse. Recast's author gives the one primary rule: the tile cache "is best for simple obstacles like crates and barrels", while "if your house has multiple floors, then you should use the regular Recast process to rebuild a tile", with tile size tuned so "the rebuild times are not too long" ([recastnavigation group](https://groups.google.com/g/recastnavigation/c/F9mRO_eimEI)). Unreal users report navmesh gaps after partial Chaos destruction, Geometry Collections that did not affect the navmesh at all in 4.25, and a 0.5 s hitch on regeneration ([UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/geometrycollection-updating-the-navmesh/884232); [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/navmesh-regeneration-lag/451257)). **These are forum reports**, consistent across threads but not verified.

The design consequence is a hard rule: **tier B debris never feeds tier A navigation**. Only the tier A rubble footprint does. For mass entities, a rubble footprint intersecting a lane becomes a lane-graph edit (`blocked` or reduced capacity), reusing M6's path invalidation. For actor navigation, Recast tiles should nest inside 256 m cells, so a collapse touches a bounded set of tiles in line with D79's bounded work, with a per-frame tile-rebuild budget. Small transient blockers such as a wrecked car use tile-cache obstacles. Cover points are derived data and are regenerated per cell from the part states, not patched. In the strategy mode, destruction is simply a sim state on the building tables (damaged, ruined, cleared, rebuilt), as in Cities: Skylines, and physics plays no role.

### Networking and replays: the save delta is the network snapshot

D41 writes no network code, but the shape already fits the Teardown hybrid. Destruction events are consequences of `player_cmd` and are replicated or run in lockstep like any other command result. Debris is local and cosmetic, apart from the few gameplay-sized chunks discussed above. A late joiner receives cell deltas exactly as a save load does, so **the save delta and the network snapshot should be one format**, which removes Teardown's capped join queue. The strategy lockstep path stays open only if tier A outcomes alone affect anything but visuals. For `.wzr` replays, damage states, lane blocks and rubble selections go into the tier A simulation hash. Debris transforms get a separate tier B hash that is reported but not gated. The CI assertion that guards the whole design is simple: **running with debris physics disabled produces identical tier A hashes**. It mirrors the existing cosmetic-scatter rule.

### Rendering: masks, part swaps and instanced debris without bindless

Frostbite 2 masked damage with a signed volume distance field built from artist-placed spheres, at about **2 m per texel**, typically **8×8×8** textures, sampled per pixel ([Kihl 2010](https://www.advances.realtimerendering.com/s2010/Kihl-Destruction%20in%20Frostbite(SIGGRAPH%202010%20Advanced%20RealTime%20Rendering%20Course).pdf)). SDL_GPU has no bindless and 16 samplers per stage, so the Wallhammer version drops the 3D texture. A short per-instance array of damage spheres (up to about 8 `float4`) in a storage buffer indexed by instance id is evaluated as a minimum distance in the pixel shader and reveals a crack layer from the material texture array. A per-vertex damage channel carries soot and char gradients. States 2–4 are new part meshes from the generator, kept in the same vertex and index buffers and material arrays as intact parts, so draws grow with distinct damaged meshes, not with chunks. Debris from one fracture shares a material and a few templates and is drawn instanced. A damaged building invalidates its HLOD and impostor. The old impostor keeps showing, with the damage-sphere mask applied, until the cell's HLOD is regenerated asynchronously from the new part states. The M3b output contract already demands "separately cullable parts"; destruction needs those to be the same parts as in `destruct_desc`. Interior reveal is the open rendering question. A breach shows the room behind the wall, which ties destruction to the "Interiors" research gap and M10's lazy interiors. The generator can emit back-face content only when a part reaches state 2 or higher.

### Audio: precomputed material banks and a hard voice budget

Physically based fracture sound is feasible at game cost once per-fragment modal models are replaced by precomputed proxies. Zheng and James smashed a glass table setting into 300+ pieces using 4,046 ellipsoidal modal proxies at roughly 500× speedup ([Cornell FractureSound](https://research.cs.cornell.edu/FractureSound)). For Wallhammer, `eng_gen_audio` (already planned to generate modal impacts in M3d) precomputes a small bank per material and size class. The tier B2 mix triggers voices from collision events under a hard budget, the loudest N per frame plus one "rubble bed" loop for the rest. Battlefield 6 drives VFX and audio from the same debris-activation and shockwave parameters ([EA](https://www.ea.com/news/how-battlefield-6-redefined-destruction)), which suggests one event stream feeding `eng_vfx` and `eng_audio`. No source gives industry voice budgets for debris.

### Testing: the existing machinery covers most of it

No studio describes automated destruction tests. DICE's "big red button", which triggers all destruction on a map at once to test each map state, is the closest thing, and it exposed performance problems because maps "are not designed to have every instance destroyed at once" ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/battlefield/battlefield-6-devs-have-a-big-button-that-blows-up-everything-on-a-map-but-it-isnt-just-for-fun-dice-uses-it-to-make-sure-maps-are-fun-in-all-three-different-states-of-destruction/)). Wallhammer can make that a headless CI scene. The machinery is already planned: golden hashes of `destruct_desc` on 3 OS, a local-stability test extended to "destroying a building changes nothing outside its cell, margin and declared neighbourhood", the version-bump report listing destruction orphans, and a save/load round trip in the middle of a collapse.

### Performance: budgets must be measured, because nobody publishes them

No primary source gives a millisecond budget, a debris cap or a cleanup timer for any title. The only hard data are mechanisms (sleep removal, network prioritization, cluster-level caps) and Teardown's memory numbers. Budgets therefore become `eng_prof` counters with values set by measurement: awake debris bodies (global and per cell), debris lifetime before rubble conversion, destruction events processed per tick (overflow deferred, never dropped, because events are tier A), nav tiles rebuilt per frame, live fractured parts per cell, and debris instances, draws and voices.

## Where the evidence is weak

Several claims that circulate about destruction rest on thin ground, and the recommendations above avoid depending on them. **The Finals**: every mechanism detail is press paraphrase, and "off-the-shelf" UE5 is a journalist's word, not Embark's. **No Man's Sky edit limits**: player forums and a guide site only. **Unreal navmesh behaviour after Chaos destruction**: forum posts. **Siege replicating a fracture seed**: explicit forum speculation ([UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/procedural-destruction-rainbow-six-siege-style/81885)). **Control's mechanism** (Northlight logic, PhysX rigid bodies): a second-hand write-up; only the GDC abstract is primary. **Red Faction internals**: fan wiki and a Polycount comment; the 2009 GDC talks are members-only. **Crackdown 3's 2–4 Mbps**: unverified. **Bethesda reset timers**: a community wiki whose sources disagree on the default. **Battlefield 6**: an official but marketing-toned article with no numbers, plus a producer's claim that this destruction "is not achievable on a generic engine", which is untested. **Teardown's 10 cm voxel** and **Noita's marching-squares conversion** are folklore. On the technique side, the grammar-emitted part graph is **original design** with no published precedent, Blast's solver makes no determinism claims and has no published benchmark, the VACD paper could not be retrieved, and Manifold's cross-platform bit identity is unverified. No source at all covers navmesh or AI response to collapse in a shipped game, destruction frame budgets, or a regenerated world that saves building destruction.

## Proposed changes to ARCHITECTURE.md and ROADMAP.md

ARCHITECTURE.md should gain a short section, "Destruction: state, not geometry", stating four rules. Destruction is a per-part state edit over regeneration. Tier A decides what breaks and where rubble lies. Physics debris is cosmetic and never read by tier A. The rubble footprint is the only input to navigation, lanes and saves. Smaller edits go to existing places. The `eng_gen_arch` row gains "part graph (`destruct_desc`)". The `eng_sim` row gains "structural graph and destruction events". The `eng_phys` row gains "debris layer". Rule 7 gains "debris physics is excluded from tier A hashes". The "Asset identity, cache and edits" section gains "state edits with a coarsening fallback". The output contract gains the part list.

## Conclusion

The research turns destruction from a physics problem into an identity problem. Every hard question (what to save, what to send, what AI sees, what survives a generator change) has the same answer once the authoritative state is "part uid → damage state" and the rubble is a function of that state. Those answers all depend on things that exist only if the building generator is written with them from the start: input-space part IDs, a part graph and a per-part state input. That is why the early column of the table matters more than the destruction milestone. A generator built without them would need a rewrite and a save migration later, while the solver, fracture, debris and rendering can arrive whenever the owner wants them.

The procedural engine also has a structural edge here, as it did with hand edits. Studios spent years building tools to recover chunks, bonds and anchors from meshes, and Red Faction paid for stored shards in texture quality. Wallhammer's grammar produces that structure as a by-product and can regenerate any damaged state exactly. The open risk is not technique but evidence. No one has published destruction budgets, navmesh handling or persistence in a regenerated world, so measurements 31–36 are the actual research, and the owner should treat their numbers, not this report's precedents, as the basis for the scope of MD.
