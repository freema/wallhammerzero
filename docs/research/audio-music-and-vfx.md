# Miniaudio mixes, the GPU pool draws, the owner listens

Wallhammer Zero should build its sound in three tiers. SDL3 supplies only the audio device, as D5 says. The single-file library **miniaudio** (public domain or MIT-0, pure C) serves as the mixer, node graph, decoder and basic 3D spatializer with Doppler. A custom `eng_audio` layer adds what miniaudio lacks: virtual voices with per-category caps, occlusion by rays, reverb zones, ducking and music clocks. Effects need **one global GPU particle pool** in compute shaders, following the AMD and Wicked Engine pattern. One indirect draw per render bucket is enough, so the missing bindless support and the CPU multi-draw loop on Metal do not matter. Content is split by category. Code handles all VFX textures (SDF shapes, noise, ray-marched flipbooks, ramps) and, for audio, UI, wind, rain, creatures, horns, sirens and engines through precomputed loop sets. It is weak at footsteps, glass, crowds and music for the radio. For those, the owner decides whether to allow one-time generated AI audio with provenance, an analogue of D14 for sound. **That is not decided yet**, and the report gives the basis for both paths. AI has no ears and judges images with only limited reliability. Quality is therefore guarded by numeric lints (LUFS, true peak, loop seams, overdraw, share of blown-out pixels, timing) over headless renders to WAV, spectrograms and contact sheets. The owner approves aesthetics by blind A/B listening and comparison with references. No number for the M4 Pro in this report is measured. Budgets are estimates and in the engine they are expressed as counts (voices, particles, fragments), because SDL_GPU has no timestamp queries. The work fits into the milestone plan like this: transparent pass and bloom in M2, generators and the audio core in M3, effects runtime and car sound in M5, weather and the dense city soundscape in M6, character effects in M7, and creatures, wind and adaptive music on the planet in M9.

## Audio core: SDL3 supplies the device, miniaudio the graph, we the voices and space

### Miniaudio covers 80% of the mix in one C file

SDL3 has a solid base for audio, but it is not a game audio engine. Everything in it "revolves around `SDL_AudioStream`". Streams convert format, sample rate and channel layout, and the device "pulls data from them and mixes them". When the default output changes, logical devices move on their own. For our purposes, the key parts are the stream callback (`SDL_SetAudioStreamGetCallback`), the gain of both device and stream, and layouts from mono to 7.1. **Positioning in 3D, however, is not mentioned at all on the SDL3 audio page** ([SDL3 wiki, CategoryAudio](https://wiki.libsdl.org/SDL3/CategoryAudio)). If we stayed with SDL3 only, we would have to write the decoders, spatializer, filters and graph ourselves.

Miniaudio fills these holes. It is **a single C source file with no dependencies except the standard library**, licensed as "public domain or MIT No Attribution" at your choice ([miniaudio README](https://github.com/mackron/miniaudio)). It contains a high-level API for mixing, effects and 3D, a flexible node graph, a resource manager, WAV, FLAC and MP3 decoding, resampling and channel mapping. Sound groups work as buses with their own volume and effect chain, and the whole graph is computed in f32. Spatialization supports a listener and a source with position, direction and cone, **source velocity for Doppler**, attenuation models (inverse, linear, exponential), rolloff and multiple listeners. Filters are biquads, LPF and HPF up to the eighth order, band-pass, notch, peaking EQ and shelves. Streaming keeps **2 seconds of data per instance** in memory, in two one-second pages. Important for us is that `ma_engine` can be created with `noDevice` and that the library supports a custom VFS and a mode where the application runs the resource manager jobs itself (`jobThreadCount = 0`). The manual states the limits openly: "HRTF and environment occlusion are not supported yet". In the parts of the manual that were read, there is no reverb node and no virtual voice management ([miniaudio manual](https://miniaud.io/docs/manual/index.html)). Whether the repository contains a reverb in the `extras` folder, the research did not verify. It is only an unconfirmed recollection and should be checked before deciding.

| Library | License | What it brings | What is missing or problematic | Role in Wallhammer Zero |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SDL3 audio | zlib (already in D5) | devices, streams, conversion, output migration | 3D, decoders, graph, filters | **device only** |
| miniaudio | public domain / MIT-0, C | graph, buses, decoders, 3D with Doppler, filters, streaming, `noDevice` | reverb (not found), virtual voices, HRTF, occlusion | **mixing core** |
| SoLoud | zlib/libpng, C++ with a C API | virtual voices, 3D, sfxr-style generator, speech synthesis | the site carries © 2013–2020, maintenance uncertain ([SoLoud](https://solhsa.com/soloud/)) | source of ideas only |
| Steam Audio | Apache-2.0, v4.8.1, C API | HRTF, occlusion against geometry, reflections, precomputed propagation ([GitHub](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/steam-audio), [web](https://valvesoftware.github.io/steam-audio/)) | precomputation does not fit streamed procedural cells; macOS arm64 support not verified | optionally later behind a port |
| OpenAL Soft | LGPL (unverified) | HRTF, EFX (unverified) | LGPL forces dynamic linking, stateful API | no |
| FMOD, Wwise | proprietary (terms unverified) | snapshots, RTPC, interactive music | foreign license, GUI tools bad for AI, conflict with D6 | only a checklist of concepts |

SoLoud would be tempting for its virtual voices and sfxr, but it is C++ inside and apparently stalled. Miniaudio is pure C and actively developed. We take over the voice management idea from SoLoud and write it ourselves. A manual rewrite of all of miniaudio by Claude, on the other hand, would mean the same functionality with a higher risk of bugs.

### Device through the platform, mix without a device, so headless comes for free

The architecture from the first report links SDL3 `PRIVATE` only into `platform`, `gpu` and `app`. `eng_audio` may depend on `asset` and `platform`. A clean solution follows from that: **`eng_platform` exposes opening an audio output with a callback** (inside it `SDL_OpenAudioDeviceStream` and the get-callback), and `eng_audio` calls a read from an `ma_engine` created with `noDevice` in this callback. SDL thus remains the only layer above the device, as D5 requires. The same engine without a device in headless mode (D17) renders N seconds into a buffer or a WAV without any audio hardware. The second option is to use miniaudio's own backends (Core Audio, WASAPI, ALSA and others) and leave out SDL audio ([miniaudio manual](https://miniaud.io/docs/manual/index.html)). It is simpler, but it breaks D5 and makes the headless path less straightforward.

This gives a property that images do not have. **Of the two outputs the player perceives, only sound can be tested entirely, all the way to the final mix, without a GPU and without audio hardware.** Its golden tests will therefore run in hosted CI on all three OSes, just like the generator golden hashes, whereas graphics golden tests require a Mac or lavapipe.

### Threads: two SPSC queues and no locks in the callback

Miniaudio itself states that only `ma_node_graph_read_pcm_frames()` is guaranteed lock-free. Attaching and detaching nodes uses spinlocks, the resource manager job queue is "not 100% lock-free", and ring buffers are safe only for one producer and one consumer. The device must not be started or stopped from the callback ([miniaudio manual](https://miniaud.io/docs/manual/index.html)). The design therefore keeps these rules. The game thread writes commands (play, stop, set parameter, move listener) into an SPSC queue, and the callback drains it at the start of each block. Feedback (voice finished, levels for meters) goes through a second SPSC queue. The callback never allocates or locks. The graph is wired once at initialization and **voices are a preallocated pool of `ma_sound`**, so nothing is attached or detached during the game. Decoding and streaming run on the engine job system or on an IO thread via SDL_AsyncIO. A buffer of 256–512 samples at 48 kHz gives 5.3–10.7 ms of latency, which is enough for a game (a calculation, not a measurement). Sounds that must lead the picture are scheduled with sample accuracy. Voxel Musou played the swing sound 7–12 frames earlier than the hitbox so that the sound arrived with the picture, not after it ([voxel-musou.md §4.8](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)).

### Virtual voices are mandatory in a dense city

A dense city easily has hundreds of sound sources: cars, pedestrians, air conditioners, birds, sirens. The design therefore assumes a fixed pool of **64–96 real voices** (a proposal, not a measurement; Voxel Musou had a cap of 56, [voxel-musou.md §4.8](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). Above it stands an unlimited set of **virtual voices**, that is, emitters that remember position, priority and an estimate of loudness, but do not compute DSP. Every tick they are sorted by `priority × estimated loudness at the listener` and promoted and demoted accordingly. Categories have caps (for example 12 car engines, 8 footsteps, 6 horns, 1 police siren of a given type), a voice stealing policy (oldest, quietest, reject new) and a minimum spacing between repeats of the same sound. All numbers are a default proposal to be tuned.

Spatial properties are composed from ready-made parts and cheap approximations. Distance and Doppler come from miniaudio. Cars need Doppler, and its factor must be limited so that a teleport or camera cut does not cause a wail. **Occlusion** is a cheap approximation: 1–3 rays from listener to source on the job system at 10–20 Hz, with the result smoothed into a per-voice LPF and gain. The default "through a wall" is an LPF around 800 Hz and −12 dB, "around a corner" only a mild LPF. Since the `audio` and `phys` modules do not link to each other according to the first report, `runtime` relays the rays through a narrow port. **Reverb** we write ourselves as a miniaudio node: an FDN or Freeverb, or possibly a convolution with an IR generated by code from noise, as in Voxel Musou. It will sit on 2–4 send buses (street, tunnel, interior, open landscape), which cross-fade by the weights of the listener's zones. Zones can be derived from cell metadata, for example from the width and height of a street canyon. This is a natural extension of D8: **the acoustic description of a cell is another output of `gen_world`**, not hand-drawn trigger boxes.

The buses form a tree master → {music, sfx (world → vehicles, peds, weapons, ambience), ui, voice/radio}. **Ducking** dims music and ambience when a priority bus sounds (dialogue, a big explosion, a hit tick as in Voxel Musou). A control envelope with attack and release is enough, a full sample-level sidechain is not needed. Mix snapshots (pause, underwater, interior, slow motion) are tables of bus gains and filters, interpolated over time. The final chain can adopt the Voxel Musou structure: EQ → compressor with a 25 ms attack → soft tanh clip ([voxel-musou.md §4.8](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). Under D21, however, all of it is rewritten in C. Nothing is taken from JS.

Two rules follow from the previous reports. First, **emitter positions are in doubles** and go into miniaudio only as floats relative to the listener. The listener's "up" orientation on a planet is given by the world-shape port, not by the constant `(0, 1, 0)`. Second, **sound never influences the simulation**. Variation RNG is seeded from the event ID and emitter ID, so the headless render is bit-for-bit repeatable on the same platform. This fixes the `Math.random` from Voxel Musou ([voxel-musou.md](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)).

## Code handles wind, creatures and UI, but not pavement, glass and crowds

### The sound category decides, not the sound as a whole

Synthesis by code is excellent for stylized and parametric sounds and weak where the listener expects "recorded reality". The boundary runs through categories, just as for graphics it ran between buildings and faces. No Man's Sky used physically modeled synthesis for creature voices. At GDC 2017, Paul Weir described the development of **VocAlien**, "a plugin for synthesis of a physically modeled vocal tract" ([GDC Vault: The Sound of No Man's Sky](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024067/The-Sound-of-No-Man)). Our own precedent, Voxel Musou, produced usable screams, grunts and layered impacts purely by code: formants from a saw through four band-pass filters, aspiration, and an impact composed of the layers click, crack, thump and sub-drop ([voxel-musou.md §4.8](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). SoLoud carries an sfxr-style generator and a speech synthesizer, so in-engine synthesis is an established pattern ([SoLoud](https://solhsa.com/soloud/)).

| Category | Recommended path | Why |
|---|---|---|
| UI, HUD, feedback | **A** code (sfxr style, pre-baked) | consistent palette, parametric; the quality ceiling does not matter |
| Wind, rain, water, fire, thunder | **A** live synthesis (filtered noise, granular drops, noise bursts with long reverb) | continuous parameters from weather; Farnell-style recipes work (the book was not verified in this research) |
| Creatures on the planet | **A** formant / vocal-tract synthesizer seeded by species | every procedural species gets its own voice; VocAlien precedent |
| Horns, sirens | **A** oscillators | simple sources, synthesis sounds faithful |
| Car engines | **A or hybrid**: sets of RPM × load loops per archetype; later a live model for the player's car | proven runtime with loop cross-fading |
| Impacts, physical collisions | **hybrid**: modal synthesis from physics + samples for glass and rubble | a modal bank fits the parameters from Jolt; shattering synthesizes poorly |
| Footsteps | **hybrid**: samples per surface + variation by code; synthesis as a fallback | synthetic footsteps sound "gamey", the GTA III bar wants the texture of a recording |
| Tires, doors | **B** samples | texture hard to synthesize |
| City ambience (crowd, hum, air conditioning) | **B** loops + A point emitters | a crowd will not convincingly come out of code |
| Voices and dialogue | out of scope (TTS is a separate topic) | |

"B" in the table means a sample, whether from an AI generator, a recording or a human. If the owner does not allow AI audio, the B categories either stay stylized (synthesis) or will need another source of samples. For the GTA III and WoW bars, which both stood on recorded samples (general knowledge, not a citation), this means: **pure code reaches the bar or beats it for UI, weather, creatures and sci-fi elements. For cars it is acceptable with synthetic loops and very good with an engine-sim class model. For footsteps, glass, crowds and spoken word it stays clearly below it.**

### Cars: loops by RPM, not an engine simulation for every car

Game practice for engines does not synthesize. The most widespread approach is cross-fading loops by RPM: idle and several RPM loops with pitch shift and with separate layers under load and off load. Racing games have over a hundred WAV files per car. Granular approaches analyze recordings offline and are cheap at runtime, but hold steady RPM less well and the interpolation "can sound quite synthetic" ([Designing Sound 2014](https://designingsound.org/2014/08/11/vehicle-engine-design-project-cars-forza-motorsport-5-and-rev/), [Audiokinetic](https://www.audiokinetic.com/engine-sound-modeling-from-sampling-to-granular-synthesis-in-wwise), [AudioMotors review](https://designingsound.org/2013/04/audiomotors-review/)). The sources are from 2013–2014 and the research did not confirm current practice. The opposite pole is **engine-sim** by AngeTheGreat, "a real-time internal combustion engine simulation designed for engine sound". It is under MIT, but "for now it builds only on Windows", depends on SDL2, Boost and Flex/Bison, and the author stresses that it is "NOT a scientific tool" ([engine-sim](https://github.com/ange-yaghi/engine-sim)). The community edition offers only release zips and a "Convolution Level" control for the exhaust IR ([community edition](https://github.com/Engine-Simulator/engine-sim-community-edition)).

The recommendation combines both. **An offline generator produces, for every engine archetype (inline four, V8, scooter, truck, electric whine), a set of RPM × load loops** by synthesis inspired by engine-sim: cylinder pulses by firing order, the exhaust as delay lines and convolution with a body IR. At runtime the loops are only cross-faded, so it is cheap for dozens of cars in traffic. Engine archetypes pair naturally with the car archetypes from M5. A live model for the player's car and the 2–3 nearest cars is a later choice, because the per-instance cost of engine-sim is not measured anywhere and its core may not be separable from the application. The engine-sim code is not taken over, it is only a reference.

### Bake offline, run live only what changes continuously

Most synthesized sounds should be **baked offline** (at asset build or first launch) into PCM banks cached by hash, exactly as D8 does with textures. Voxel Musou baked the whole bank at startup ([voxel-musou.md §4.8](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). Only sources with continuous parameters will run live: wind, rain density, engines and creature voices with per-instance variation. The load of the audio thread thus stays predictable. This yields a requirement that the notes do not state explicitly. **Baked audio generators (`eng_gen_audio`) must follow the same determinism rules as `gen_tex`**: math from `gen_core` instead of `<math.h>`, hash RNG and golden hashes on three OSes. Otherwise sound caches would diverge between platforms. Live synthesis on the audio thread does not need such a guarantee, because it stores nothing.

### AI audio in 2026: sound effects mature, music legally shaky

Sound effects models are good enough for one-time generation of source assets. **ElevenLabs `eleven_text_to_sound_v3`** handles multi-level prompts, a length of 0.1–30 s, seamless loops and 48 kHz WAV for non-loop effects. It costs 40 credits per second if the length is specified. The older v2 is deprecated and **"will be removed on 1 March 2027"**. The documentation does not address commercial use ([ElevenLabs docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/product/sound-effects/overview)). Secondary sources disagree. One blog from May 2026 claims that commercial use requires a paid Starter plan or higher and that selling or redistributing the effects themselves is forbidden, while embedding them in a game is fine. Another site speaks of royalty-free outputs ([Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/articles/elevenlabs-sound-effects-web-ui-trial/), [ScriptByAI](https://www.scriptbyai.com/elevenlabs-sound-effect)). An open alternative is **Stable Audio Open 1.0** under the Stability AI Community License: up to 47 s of stereo at 44.1 kHz, trained on roughly 486 thousand recordings under CC0, CC BY or CC Sampling+ (472,618 from Freesound, 13,874 from Free Music Archive). It is "better at sound effects and field recordings than at music" and cannot do realistic voices ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-audio-open-1.0)). The revenue cap of the license (recollection: 1 million USD) the research did not verify.

Music is a different story. After UMG's settlement with Udio in October 2025, downloading from Udio was restricted ([AP via Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/ai-song-generator-udio-offers-132621881.html)), and in September 2026 Udio reportedly does not allow downloading audio or stems on any plan. Warner settled with Suno in November 2025, which is to launch licensed models ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/?p=722896)). Suno Pro includes commercial rights and downloads, the free tier does not, and Sony's claims reportedly remain open ([Dubspot](https://blog.dubspot.com/suno-udio-ai-music-lawsuits-2026), [AI Law Wiki](https://ailawwiki.com/News_Major_Labels_Settle_Suno_Udio_2026)). One analyst points out that licenses after the settlement do not retroactively clean up tracks from older models ([Sonilo](https://sonilo.com/blog/comparisons/udio-v4-warner-music-licensing-explained)). **The legal risk of AI audio is therefore concentrated in music, not in effects.**

On 16 January 2026, Steam changed its content questionnaire. It requires disclosure of AI content that is "consumed by players", while efficiency tools (for example code assistants) are exempt. The questionnaire distinguishes **pre-generated** content, which falls under the same rules as other content, from content **generated at runtime**, for which the developer must describe safeguards ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/steam-updates-ai-disclosure-form-to-specify-that-its-focused-on-ai-generated-content-that-is-consumed-by-players-not-efficiency-tools-used-behind-the-scenes/), [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260120-steam-updates-ai-disclosure-guidelines)). The research did not read Valve's own text. For the owner's decision, an essential point follows. **A game that uses AI textures under D14 will disclose AI content on Steam anyway.** AI audio would therefore change the scope of the disclosure, not its category. Runtime generation is not planned. Claude's code as an efficiency tool does not trigger disclosure, and procedural audio from code does not need it at all.

If the owner allowed AI audio, it should mirror D14. Every audio asset declares a source: `proc` (generator, parameters, seed), or `ai` (model ID and version, prompt, seed if available, length, loop flag, date, a snapshot of the plan terms, hash of the raw file). **The raw output is committed to the repo as a source asset**, because generation cannot be repeated. For ElevenLabs this holds doubly, because the v2 model disappears in four months. Code then always processes the result: trim and fade, DC removal, EBU R128 normalization to the category target, a true-peak limiter, loop seam repair (a crossfade at a zero crossing, even if the model claims the loop is seamless), resampling to 48 kHz, mono for 3D emitters and slicing into variation sets. At runtime, code adds variability: pitch ±3–8%, filter jitter, layer recombination and round-robin. AI generators drift between prompts, so three measures are needed. A fixed prompt template with style tokens and "dry, close-mic, no reverb", because the runtime supplies the reverb. Many candidates per slot and selection by analysis. And a shared EQ and compression chain for the whole set as an **audio palette**, an analogue of palette remapping for textures. Against D6, there is a practical difference between the two models. ElevenLabs is a cloud API called through `curl`, which the system already has. Stable Audio Open would run locally, but it needs a Python and PyTorch environment in `.tools/` with several GB of weights and libraries (estimate).

## Music: Pulse instead of a composer, the radio stays open

For an infinite procedural world, the proven pattern is **Pulse** from No Man's Sky. People (65daysofstatic) composed a library of music fragments, and a system of rules assembles infinite "soundscapes" from them according to the game state. The band could not try Paul Weir's tool, so it imitated it with ordinary tools and built a prototype to his specification ([MusicRadar](https://musicradar.com/news/guitars/how-65daysofstatic-built-the-soundtrack-to-no-mans-skys-infinite-universe-641184), [GDC Vault 1024067](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024067/The-Sound-of-No-Man)). The second precedent is **Spore** with fully generative music in a modified Pure Data (EApd). Brian Eno spent a week with the team, and the key step was "teaching the system to filter notes into scales and modes so that it starts to sound like music and not like noise". The creators later said that the game used "only a fraction of the potential" of the system ([archive.org GDC2008](https://archive.org/details/GDC2008Jolly), [PdCon](https://www.uni-weimar.de/kunst-und-gestaltung/wiki/PDCON:Conference/Using_Pure_Data_in_Spore_and_Darkspore), [interview with Eno](https://moredarkthanshark.org/eno_int_gspy-feb08.html)). Both cases show the same thing: generative music is good when the fragment material and the harmonic rules are curated. The research did not obtain Pulse's internal rules, because Weir's talk is a paid video.

The engine therefore needs a **music system that is agnostic about the content source**. A fragment is a PCM track with metadata (key, tempo, number of bars, intensity, tags). The `music` system in `eng_audio` has five parts, all derived from the interactive music concepts of FMOD and Wwise (general knowledge, not a citation). A sample-accurate music clock quantizes transitions to a beat, a bar or a marker. Horizontal resequencing uses segments with entry and exit points and a transition matrix of the type "from state X to state Y via bridge Z at the next bar". Vertical layering mutes and raises tracks (pad, rhythm, melody, percussion) according to parameters such as danger, speed, time of day and biome. Stingers are one-shot sounds aligned to the beat. Pulse-style rule-based selection takes fragments from weighted pools with constraints (key, tempo, density, no repetition for N minutes) and **is seeded by the position in the world or the planet seed, so a place sounds like itself every time**. Music thereby adopts the NMS principle "recompute from the seed" from the previous report.

A GTA-style radio is a different system and technically simpler. Each station is a long linear playlist that **virtually keeps running even when the player is not listening**, with DJ and ad inserts. From outside the car it plays through a "car radio" filter. The engine needs one streamed voice and a virtual clock per station for this. The content is the heavy part: tens of minutes of finished tracks per station. A sequencer with its own synthesizers (chiptune, ambient, drone, simple electronics) can reach stylistically coherent music, and generative ambient from code is a credible target for the planet. **Produced genres for a radio (rock, pop, hip-hop) cannot be composed by code at a reasonable level**, because they need sampled instruments and mixing craft. For the radio, that leaves AI music with legal risk, human or licensed music, or a deliberately stylized "radio from code". The owner decides this.

## Effects: one GPU pool, five buckets, one indirect draw per bucket

### The AMD and Wicked Engine pattern maps onto SDL_GPU without remainder

The proven GPU particle pattern leads from Gareth Thomas's AMD talk at GDC 2014 ([GDC Vault 1020002](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020002/Advanced-Visual-Effects-with-DirectX)) to Wicked Engine, which still uses it in master today. The research read Wicked's source code directly. A particle is 64 bytes (position, mass, force, packed rotation, velocity, lifetime, size at start and end, color). The counter buffer holds `aliveCount`, `deadCount`, `realEmitCount`, `aliveCount_afterSimulation` and `culledCount` ([ShaderInterop_EmittedParticle.h](https://github.com/turanszkij/WickedEngine/blob/master/WickedEngine/shaders/ShaderInterop_EmittedParticle.h)). The simulation reads the alive list, atomically appends survivors to a new list, returns dead ones to the dead list and writes visible ones to the culled list after a frustum test. For sorting it writes `-asuint(distSQ)`, a trick of "negation instead of rewriting the sort shaders" ([emittedparticle_simulateCS.hlsl](https://github.com/turanszkij/WickedEngine/blob/master/WickedEngine/shaders/emittedparticle_simulateCS.hlsl)). The key is a pair of tiny **single-thread** passes. Kickoff computes the group count for the dispatch and swaps the counters ([kickoffUpdateCS](https://github.com/turanszkij/WickedEngine/blob/master/WickedEngine/shaders/emittedparticle_kickoffUpdateCS.hlsl)), and finish writes the indirect draw arguments `{4 vertices, culledCount instances}` ([finishUpdateCS](https://github.com/turanszkij/WickedEngine/blob/master/WickedEngine/shaders/emittedparticle_finishUpdateCS.hlsl)). **The CPU therefore never reads counts back from the GPU.**

Wicked, however, allocates buffers for each emitter separately. That means N sets of passes (emit, kickoff, simulate, finish, draw), which suits an editor but not a city with 200 live effects. The design for Wallhammer Zero therefore uses **one global pool** (for example 512k–1M slots × 48–64 B ≈ 32–64 MB) with a shared dead list. Each particle carries a 16-bit `emitter_type` and a 16-bit `instance`. The type parameters (curves, flipbook layer range, blend bucket, forces) live in one `StructuredBuffer<EmitterType>` and the instance state in another. A whole frame is then one emit dispatch, one kickoff, one simulation, zero or one sort, one finish and **one `SDL_DrawGPUPrimitivesIndirect(draw_count = 1)` per render bucket** ([SDL3 wiki](https://wiki.libsdl.org/SDL3/SDL_DrawGPUPrimitivesIndirect)). Because each indirect call draws only once, it does not matter that multi-draw indirect is a CPU loop on Metal (D5).

Buckets replace drawing per emitter. **Solid** contains alpha-clip particles and rubble as meshes, writes depth and casts shadows. **Premul** is premultiplied alpha for fire, smoke, sparks and magic. Additive blending is just premultiplied with α = 0, so additive and transparent particles share one blend state and one draw. **Distortion** writes into an offset buffer at half resolution. **Halfres** is bulky smoke at half resolution. **Ribbon** serves trails and skids with their own topology. The missing bindless is solved with a texture array: flipbooks are generated by code in fixed sizes, so they all fit into one `Texture2DArray` (for example 512² layers in BC7/ASTC with an 8×8 or 4×4 flipbook per layer). Size, color and alpha curves over life as well as color ramps live in one "ramp atlas" with a row per ramp, and it fits the engine's palette style. The threejs-punk analysis already showed earlier that GPU rain needs 2–4 RW storage buffers (the SDL_GPU limit is 8, taken from our own note, not re-verified) and one texture, and that neither bindless nor indirect is necessary ([threejs-punk.md](notes/reference-repos/threejs-punk.md)).

### Sorting off, smoke at half resolution

Chapter 23 of GPU Gems 3 brought the only truly measured numbers in this area, even if on 2007 hardware. On a GeForce 8800 GTX at 1600×1200, full resolution ran at 25 fps with 46.9 M particle pixels. Off-screen rendering at 1/4×1/4 gave 61 fps at 2.9 M and 1/2×1/2 gave 46 fps at 11.7 M. Depth is downscaled with the maximum of 4 samples. Alpha particles need a separate alpha blend, and the result "will never be completely free of artifacts", so the technique suits soft smoke and fog, not rubble. Soft particles use `alpha *= saturate(scale * (sceneDepth − particleDepth))` ([GPU Gems 3 ch. 23](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-iv-image-effects/chapter-23-high-speed-screen-particles)). Unreal Niagara has sorting off by default, because it costs GPU time and memory for indices ([Niagara best practices](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/scalability-and-best-practices-for-niagara)).

For the stylized look, this yields the policy **"do not sort unless needed"**. Effect instances are sorted back to front on the CPU, which is cheap for tens to hundreds of instances, and only for the halfres bucket. Inside an instance, premultiplied alpha, a few large shapes and a spawn order that roughly matches age are enough. A GPU bitonic sort is switched on only for types with dense lingering smoke. As a "never sort" fallback for the halfres bucket, **Weighted Blended OIT** is offered. It uses RGBA16F accumulation and an R8 "revealage" and is explicitly suited to low-resolution particles and smoke, but its weight function must be tuned and it cannot do refraction ([Casual Effects](https://casual-effects.blogspot.com/2014/03/weighted-blended-order-independent.html)). The choice between sorting and WBOIT is technical and will be made in M6 based on measurements.

The lighting of most stylized effects is **unlit plus emission or ramp tinting** (fire, sparks, magic, muzzle flash). Smoke and dust must sit in the scene. For a particle, hemispherical ambient light from the same ramp as the world, sun `N·L` with a spherical normal and ≤ 4 nearest point lights are therefore enough, all quantized through the project ramp. DOOM 2016 computes particle lighting into tiles of a 4K atlas and then upscales it ([Courrèges, DOOM study](https://www.adriancourreges.com/blog/2016/09/09/doom-2016-graphics-study/)), which is unnecessary for our style.

### Stylization is five shader blocks and Riot's rules as lints

Stylized VFX converge to a small set of rules that can be written into code. The League of Legends style guide (2017, art director Jin Ho Yang) sets four goals: gameplay readability, minimum clutter, support of the character's theme and surprise. The importance of an effect is governed by size, shape, timing, value, saturation and coverage ([VFX Apprentice](https://www.vfxapprentice.com/blog/10-league-of-legends-vfx-design-tips)). A secondary summary turns the guide into concrete rules. Every effect has a primary element (high value, clear silhouette, high coverage) and secondary elements that "never compete" with it. No pure black or white except an instant flash. Environment saturation is the "floor of readability" and effects have a higher ceiling. One dominant hue. No photographic textures or noisy procedural detail. Timing is nonlinear: anticipation, then impact "fully active in the first quarter of the effect's life", then a smooth fade. And effects should be short, because "lingering particles cause serious overdraw" (same source). Riot's original PDF is unavailable today, the rules come from a summary that cites it.

Technically, stylization rests on **five shader blocks** that cover most effects. `mask(shape_id)` takes a shape from an SDF or from a shape array. `erode(mask, threshold, feather)` is threshold dissolve. The threshold range must be widened to `[0 − feather, 1 + feather]` and masks must be linear, not sRGB. Dithered dissolve through a Bayer matrix gives pseudo-transparency without overdraw ([vfxdoc: alpha erosion](https://vfxdoc.readthedocs.io/en/latest/shaders/alpha-erosion/)). `pan_distort(uv, noise_id, speed, strength)` scrolls and warps UVs with noise. `gradient_map(value, ramp_id)` colors with a ramp from the palette. `step_time(t, fps)` quantizes time so the animation looks like a hand-drawn flipbook. The same trick is used by Mirza Beig's fully procedural stylized fire without a single flipbook ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/check-out-this-fully-procedural-stylized-fire-vfx-made-with-shaders)). Fire is then vertically scrolled noise times a droplet-shaped SDF, eroded by age and colored with a ramp black → red → orange → yellow → white in 3–5 hard steps. Smoke is round puffs eroded by cellular noise and lit with a two-tone ramp. Simon Trümpler showed stylized fire in RiME made from simple textures with UV distortion (the talk page is unavailable, only a summary, [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/talks-and-demos-stylized-vfx-in-rime-by-simon-trumpler-unreal-fest-europe-2018-unreal-engine/610939)).

Flipbooks are created by code in three classes, and all are "from code" under D8. The cheapest are 2D animations from SDFs: an expanding circle, a splash crown, a star burst, lightning as a polyline, all with domain-warped noise and quantization to palette steps. The second class is tileable 3D noises (Perlin, Worley, curl) baked into a 32³–64³ volume for scrolling. The third is **volumetric ray-marching** of a density field (spheres and FBM over time, possibly a small offline smoke solver) from a fixed view into an 8×8 flipbook with the channels density, light from one or two directions, and temperature or emission. At runtime, the temperature is then colored with a ramp and the density lit. Battlefront II layers smoke and emissive fire as separate flipbook layers that a LUT colors consistently during playback ([engineered.at](https://engineered.at/articles/battlefront-ii-layered-explosion), summary only). The third class thus gives the same result without a DCC tool. The rule for choosing: **a procedural shader for everything that loops endlessly** (torches, campfires, neon flicker, magic auras), **a flipbook for one-shot complex shapes** (fireball, smoke puff, splash crown). AI images under D14 could be used for a small sheet of "painted brush strokes" in the WoW style. They would, however, have to go through the same path as other textures (conversion to grayscale masks, linear data, the ramp applied only at runtime). The default choice is masks from code.

The emission budget is also the brightness budget. Threejs-punk draws bloom with threshold 0 only from an **emissive MRT**, so only what the material declares as emissive glows, and fog dampens the bloom (`bloom·(1 − fog·0.75)`) ([threejs-punk.md §6](notes/reference-repos/threejs-punk.md)). Voxel Musou shows what happens without a budget: additive musou waves cover 1/3 to 1/2 of the screen with white, yellow or green and the scene disappears ([voxel-musou.md §7, §8.8](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). This is the source of the lint on brightness and screen coverage.

### Decals, skids, distortion, weather, rubble and lights from particles

**Decals** should be projected as volumes in the main pass. DOOM 2016 applies them while drawing opaque meshes in the clustered forward pass from an atlas, and each of 3,072 clusters (16×8×24) references up to 256 decals ([Courrèges](https://www.adriancourreges.com/blog/2016/09/09/doom-2016-graphics-study/)). Screen-space decals from Warhammer 40,000: Space Marine arose because decals cut from mesh pieces z-fight and stretch ([SIGGRAPH 2012](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=140176)). Because the second report chooses a deferred G-buffer, decals modify albedo, normal and roughness in a deferred decal pass. Textures live in one `Texture2DArray` (bullet holes as an SDF crater with cracks, scorch marks from noise and a radial gradient, stains as metaballs) and are stored in ring buffers per category (for example 512 bullet holes, 64 scorch marks) with eviction of the oldest and a fade. **Skid marks** are not decals but a ribbon per wheel in a ring buffer (for example 4 wheels × 512 segments) laid on the road with a small depth offset. Width and alpha are driven by the slip. The same ribbon code serves weapon trails, magic, car lights and glowing projectiles. Voxel Musou samples a weapon trail from a **pure pose function** without history, so it can be sampled at 120+ Hz too ([voxel-musou.md §4.5–4.6](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)).

**Distortion** (heat shimmer over fire, shock wave, exhaust) is drawn into one RG16F offset target at half resolution, masked by depth, and read by the final post pass. DOOM does it the same way: R and G are the offset, B is the blur, and bloom runs at 1/4 resolution ([Courrèges](https://www.adriancourreges.com/blog/2016/09/09/doom-2016-graphics-study/)).

**Weather** takes over the collision rain from threejs-punk, but with fixes. An orthographic camera from above draws a 512² height map over 100×100 m (≈ 0.195 m per texel) centered on the player. 5,000 drops in a 60×60 m volume move 15 m in front of the camera with toroidal wrap. Splashes are not tied to drop impacts, each of the 5,000 instances appears 6 times per second at a random XZ with the height from the map. The analysis found bugs: integration without `dt` (at 120 Hz the rain falls twice as fast), a seed `float(index) + time·1000` that loses precision over hours, and an ortho camera at a height of 50 m, so in skyscrapers it rains through roofs ([threejs-punk.md §1](notes/reference-repos/threejs-punk.md)). Our `weather` module therefore integrates with `dt`, seeds with a uint PCG hash with a frame counter, draws into a `D32_FLOAT` depth target from a height above the tallest building and targets 30–100 thousand drops (an estimate for the M4 Pro). **The same map serves five purposes**: drop collision, splashes, dry strips under awnings and bridges in wetness, snow accumulation and off-screen particle collision with the floor. Wicked has a "rain blocker" emitter flag for the same thing ([ShaderInterop](https://github.com/turanszkij/WickedEngine/blob/master/WickedEngine/shaders/ShaderInterop_EmittedParticle.h)). On a planet, the "down" direction for rain and snow is given by the world-shape port, and the map is drawn along the local "up". Fog starts as analytic volumes per cell, froxels only later. Lightning is a polyline from midpoint displacement as a ribbon, plus a sky flash for 2–3 frames, a short directional light and **thunder delayed by distance** in the audio.

**Rubble** takes over the Voxel Musou constants as GPU particles in the solid bucket: gravity 22 m/s², bounce −0.32 on impact below −1.5 m/s, horizontal damping ×0.55, settling and persistence, rotation proportional to 0.16/size, and a count by distance and load ([voxel-musou.md §4.5](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). Pieces that matter to the game (they block a car) belong to Jolt. **Lights from particles** are a small prioritized budget on the CPU, the proposal is ≤ 32 dynamic VFX lights in the cluster structure. The rest relies on emission and bloom. Threejs-punk has no point lights at all, and a night neon city still reads as lit ([threejs-punk.md §7](notes/reference-repos/threejs-punk.md)).

### The game computes on the CPU, effects are merely reproducible

The split must be hard. Everything that changes the game (a projectile hit, the damage radius of an explosion, rubble that blocks cars) lives in the CPU simulation with a fixed step and in Jolt. GPU effects are cosmetic and need only **reproducibility for golden frames**: a fixed 60 Hz step with `dt`, a seed `hash(seed_instance, spawn_counter)` from the `gen_core` family and never `time·1000`. Headless capture runs with interpolation 0. Wicked has a fixed emitter step option, because without it the simulation "explodes" ([ShaderInterop](https://github.com/turanszkij/WickedEngine/blob/master/WickedEngine/shaders/ShaderInterop_EmittedParticle.h)). Voxel Musou ages effects at half speed during hitstop, so sparks "hang" with the hit ([voxel-musou.md §4.5](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)). Golden comparison of effects must be perceptual with tolerance, not bitwise, because the order of float atomics differs between backends. Wicked (MIT, C++ and HLSL) serves as a source of ideas. Shaders are written anew, and if anything were taken over verbatim, a license note must remain. Under D21, no code is taken from threejs-punk and Voxel Musou.

## An AI author without ears and with an unreliable eye: lints, sheets and blind A/B

The third report showed that a model as a verifier of renders agreed with humans in BlenderGym in only **0.66** of cases, while humans agreed with each other in 0.79 ([BlenderGym](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01786)). For audio even this level is missing, because Claude does not hear sound at all. The research found no published practice of "an AI agent evaluates game audio", so the loops that follow are a proposal built on D17 and D18. Both domains share one principle: **the agent writes data and programs, the engine returns numbers and images, lints guard what is measurable, and the owner approves taste.**

### An effect is a file with a typed schema, modules are code

Unreal Niagara models an effect as System → Emitters with ordered modules in the spawn and update stages ([Niagara overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/overview-of-niagara-effects-for-unreal-engine)). Reusable **Effect Types** in it hold overrides by quality and platform (remove instances, scale counts, turn off renderers). Niagara also advises avoiding warm-up, spreading large bursts over several frames and merging bursts into a long-living system ([Niagara scalability](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/scalability-and-best-practices-for-niagara)). Destiny compiles parameter expressions into HLSL and into interpreter bytecode for CPU and GPU, with the goal of "sub-second iteration" ([80.lv, Destiny](https://80.lv/articles/siggraph-the-destiny-particle-architecture/)). For an engine written by AI, the right cut is this: **a fixed library of modules in the engine (C for the CPU, HLSL for the GPU) and a declarative effect file** with typed parameters with ranges, curves and only ramp IDs, no raw RGB. The schema is generated from the module registry, so the agent receives exact errors and the studio builds parameter panels from it by itself. Expressions form a small sandboxed language (constants, parameters, `rand`, curves), smaller than in Destiny. Free HLSL in effect files is not there yet.

```
effect "explosion_car_small" {
  type = "explosion"                 # Effect Type: lifetime caps, budgets, LOD, max. instances
  params { scale: float [0.5..3] = 1.0; fuel: float [0..1] = 0.5 }
  layers = [
    { name="flash",    renderer="sprite", bucket="premul", count=1, life=0.08, shape="sdf:starburst", ramp="fire_hot", emissive=4.0 },
    { name="fireball", renderer="sprite", bucket="premul", count=6, life=[0.5,0.9], flipbook="gen:fireball_v1", ramp="fire",
      size_curve="ease_out(0.6->2.4)*scale", erode_curve="lin(0->1)@[0.3..1]" },
    { name="smoke",    renderer="sprite", bucket="halfres", count=10, life=[2.5,4], flipbook="gen:smoke_puff_v1", lit="ramp_hemi", spawn_delay=0.15 },
    { name="sparks",   renderer="streak", bucket="premul", count=40*scale, life=[0.3,0.8], gravity=1, collide="depth" },
    { name="debris",   renderer="mesh",   bucket="solid",  count=8, mesh="gen:debris_clump", collide="depth|height", settle=true },
    { name="shock",    renderer="distortion", shape="sdf:ring", life=0.25 },
    { name="light",    renderer="light",  life=0.3, color_ramp="fire", radius=8*scale, priority=high },
    { name="scorch",   renderer="decal",  life=60, shape="gen:scorch_v2", size=3*scale },
    { name="shake",    renderer="camera_shake", amp=0.4*scale, falloff=25 },
    { name="sound",    renderer="audio_cue", cue="explosion_small" } ] }
```

Effect sizes may refer to the scale table from D16 (`car.length`). The last layer shows where sound and effects meet. According to the first report, `eng_vfx` and `eng_audio` are modules at the same level and do not link to each other. **The `audio_cue` layer therefore only exposes a request and `runtime` passes it to audio.** Both sides listen to the same simulation events (`hit`, `explode`, `skid`, `footstep`). The rule from Voxel Musou applies: "the simulation emits, the render listens and must not change simulation state" ([voxel-musou.md §2](notes/reference-repos/voxel-musou.md)), and so does audio. Hot reload rebuilds the emitter type records and cached textures and rebinds live instances to the new IDs. The goal is under 1 s from save to image.

### The effects loop: contact sheet, metrics, verdicts

The agent edits the effect file and calls `vfx.preview(effect, scenes, seeds)` via MCP. The engine returns three things. The first is a **contact sheet** of 8–12 frames at fixed times (0; 0.03; 0.08; 0.15; 0.25; 0.4; 0.6; 1.0; 1.5; 2.5 s) on three backgrounds (neutral gray, night street, noon), plus an overdraw heatmap and a mask of blown-out pixels in the peak frame. The second is JSON with metrics and the third is the lint verdicts. In the studio (D18) there will be a "VFX bench" with loop playback, a timeline, slow motion, hitstop simulation and views of wireframe, overdraw and coverage. The agent compares the sheet with a reference sheet from the owner, and the final approval is the owner's.

### The audio loop: numbers, spectrograms and golden scenarios

For audio, the agent replaces hearing with three tools. **Lints** measure integrated loudness per BS.1770/R128 (LUFS), true peak, loudness range, clipping, DC offset, silence at the start, reverb tail at the end, loop seam continuity and spectral descriptors within category limits. A footstep that has most of its energy above 8 kHz is flagged by a lint. The second tool is **log-mel spectrograms and waveforms as PNG**, in which the agent sees onsets, ends, the noise floor, seam discontinuities and frequency balance. The third is **golden scenarios** in headless mode, for example "a car passes the listener at 80 km/h and enters a tunnel". The render to WAV is numerically compared with the expected envelopes: the Doppler pitch curve, the LPF under occlusion, the length of the reverb tail. Against a stored golden, it is compared with tolerance on the same platform. Via MCP the engine exposes `audio.render_scenario`, `audio.lint` and `audio.meters`, plus debug counters: active and virtual voices, bus meters, stolen voices, per-voice occlusion, weights of the reverb zones and the music state.

The loudness targets have industry support. Sony ASWG-R001 sets −23 LUFS ±2 LU for consoles, −18 LUFS for handheld, true peak ≤ −1 dBTP and a range ≤ 20 LU ([Designing Sound, Garry Taylor](https://designingsound.org/2012/07/30/video-games-and-loudness-standards-interview-with-sonys-garry-taylor/)). Other recommendations give −24 LUFS for consoles and −16 for mobile devices ([IESD Mix Ref Levels](https://www.audiogang.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/IESD-Mix-Ref-Levels-v03.02.pdf)). Desktop PC has no mandate. Proposal: ambient loops −30 to −24 LUFS, the whole music mix −23 LUFS, effects measured by short-term maximum, everything ±1 LU. An implementation is offered by libebur128 (MIT, C, stated without verification in this research) vendored into `external/`, because ffmpeg would have to be installed. A semantic score via audio-text embeddings of the CLAP type could rank AI candidates, but nobody has measured its reliability for game effects.

Aesthetics are finally decided by the owner on a **blind A/B/X listening page** in the studio. The page can play a loop with a visible seam marker, shows a spectrogram, saves ratings to JSON and writes the decisions back into the provenance manifest. By role, the owner is the art director, so approving audio is his, just as with images.

| Lint | Domain | Limit (proposal to be tuned) |
|---|---|---|
| Integrated loudness | audio | category target ±1 LU |
| True peak, clipping, DC | audio | ≤ −1 dBTP; no sample ±1.0; \|DC\| < 0.001 |
| Silence at start, end | audio | < 5 ms for one-shots; end below −60 dBFS |
| Loop seam | audio | jump at the transition below threshold; spectral flux across the seam ≈ file median |
| Format | audio | 48 kHz; 3D emitters mono |
| Spectrum and length | audio | centroid and bandwidth within category limits; length within limits |
| Duplicates in a variation set | audio | flag variants that are too similar and too different |
| Overdraw | VFX | p99 and max layers per pixel within the category budget |
| Brightness | VFX | share of pixels with luminance > 0.95 after tonemap below the cap, except a short flash |
| Timing | VFX | brightness peak in the first quarter of life (Riot) |
| Lifetime | VFX | for example hit ≤ 0.6 s, fireball ≤ 1.5 s, smoke ≤ 6 s |
| Lingering transparency | VFX | no particles below ~2% coverage (needless overdraw) |
| Half resolution | VFX | a sprite layer above N% of the screen must be in halfres |
| Hue, masks | VFX | dominant hue in a palette slot; masks linear |

## Budgets on the M4 Pro: count fragments and voices, not milliseconds

No number in this section is measured on the development machine. The third report estimated the whole GPU frame at 1080p internal at 7–14 ms, and the row "sky, particles, transparency, water" at **0.6–1.2 ms**. The M4 Pro with a 16-core GPU corresponds to an RTX 4050 Laptop in modern rasterization (Steel Nomad 1620 vs. 1613) and has 273 GB/s of memory shared with the CPU ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Apple-M4-Pro-16-Core-GPU-Benchmarks-and-Specs.937474.0.html)). Particle simulation is cheap by comparison. 500 thousand particles at 64–128 B of read and write give ≈ 64 MB per frame, that is ≈ 3.8 GB/s at 60 fps, about 1.4% of the bandwidth (a calculation, not a measurement). **Filling large transparent sprites is what is expensive**, so the effects budget is counted in overdraw, not in particle count. Because SDL_GPU has no timestamp queries (D5), the engine cannot measure the time of individual passes. Budgets are therefore expressed in units the engine counts itself: live particles, particles in a bucket, fragments from an overdraw debug pass (re-rendering the buckets additively with 1/255 into R16 and a histogram) and pixels at full and half resolution. Milliseconds are verified outside the engine in Xcode GPU capture and Instruments. Discard and alpha-test can disable hidden-surface removal on TBDR GPUs. For transparent effects, blended sprites are therefore better, and for a "solid-looking" dissolve, dithered erosion (general knowledge about TBDR; Apple's page did not load).

|---|---|---|

| Off-screen particles | 1/4×1/4: 16× fewer pixels, 2.4× higher fps; 1/2×1/2: 4× fewer pixels, 1.8× higher fps (8800 GTX) | **measured** (GPU Gems 3, HW 2007) |

To the twelve measurements from the third report, to be done at the start of implementation, six more should be added. Each should be created as a permanent test or measurement scene, not as a throwaway prototype (D11). First, the CPU of the audio thread with 96 voices, filters and 4 reverb buses, and stability without dropouts at a 256-sample buffer through SDL3 on Core Audio. Second, the per-instance cost of a live engine model. Third, the cost of a million premultiplied fragments in ms at 1080p on the M4 Pro, from which the fragment budget is calibrated. Fourth, the throughput of atomic append and bitonic sort on Metal through SDL_GPU. Fifth, 100 thousand rain drops with collision against the height map. Sixth, the cost of halfres composition and WBOIT on TBDR.

## Modules and milestones: audio from M3, effects from M2, planet in M9

### Two runtime modules, two generators, nothing directly between neighbors

The notes propose splitting audio into seven targets (`eng_audio_device`, `eng_audio`, `eng_audio_dsp`, `eng_audio_synth`, `eng_gen_audio`, `eng_audio_import`, `eng_music`). The architecture from the first report, however, keeps modules coarse and puts ports only at seams. The recommendation therefore merges the runtime into a single `eng_audio` with subfolders and puts the offline part into `eng_gen_audio` on the pattern of `eng_gen_tex`, which also imports AI sources. A finer split remains as an alternative if `eng_audio` outgrows itself.


Four integration rules hold the structure together. First, `eng_audio`, `eng_vfx` and `eng_phys` do not link to each other. Simulation events, audio requests from effects and rays for occlusion are passed by `runtime` through FrameParams and narrow ports. Second, **weather state is simulation data** that belongs to the game or the world. The render (wetness), `eng_vfx` (drops, snow) and `eng_audio` (rain density, wind) only read it from FrameParams. The sound of "rain on a roof or into the street" therefore does not read the GPU map (no readback), but takes an upward ray from physics at 10 Hz. Third, cell acoustic metadata (zone type, canyon width) and point ambient emitters (air conditioners, birds, dogs) are produced by `gen_world` as part of the cell blob. Audio is thus streamed with the cell and nothing is drawn by hand. Fourth, the mapping "which event triggers which effect and sound" and the playable projectiles and rubble belong to the project's game DLL, not to the engine. The engine stays general under D15 and D19. The `audio_events` table (event → set of sounds, category, priority, cap, stealing, attenuation, bus, variation) is plain text that Claude writes just like recipes.

## Conclusion

In this report, audio and effects show up as mirror images of the same design. Both are **listeners to simulation events**, both have a runtime made of ready-made parts (miniaudio, the AMD/Wicked pattern) and their own thin layer that solves what libraries lack in an open world (virtual voices, a global pool, buckets). The content of both is a program, parameters and a seed, cached by hash and guarded by lints. The effect file with the `audio_cue` layer is where the two domains join, and because the modules do not link to each other, the join passes through `runtime`. That enforces the same discipline that holds the rest of the engine. The most valuable difference is unexpected. **Unlike images, audio can be tested entirely, all the way to the final mix, without any hardware**, so it can become the best-covered output part of the engine, even though Claude does not hear it. Numbers, spectrograms and headless golden scenarios replace hearing for it, whereas effects need a GPU and perceptual tolerances.

The quality risk sits, as with graphics, in a few content categories: footsteps, glass, crowds and radio music correspond to faces and painted textures. The legal risk of AI also concentrates in music, not in effects. For the owner, the cheapest decision is therefore to allow AI only for sound effects and ambience, with the same provenance as D14, and for now to leave music to code and curation. A game with AI textures needs the AI content disclosure anyway. The main unknowns are neither libraries nor architecture. They are two numbers that nobody has measured yet: how much a million transparent fragments cost on the M4 Pro, and how much CPU the audio thread eats with a hundred voices. All the budgets in this report stand on them, and until they are measured they remain well-reasoned estimates.
